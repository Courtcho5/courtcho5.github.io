A Foot Drop Device, From Teardown to Tested Prototype in Six Weeks

Foot drop makes stroke survivors catch their toes and fall. The clinical fix is a rigid plastic brace that works by preventing motion entirely — this was an attempt at something less blunt.

Role
Design & Human Factors Engineer
Timeline
6 weeks
Course
Design for Rehabilitation Robotics (BIOE 5520)
Institution
Northeastern University

The Condition

Foot drop is one of the most common mobility consequences of hemiplegic stroke. Damage to the motor pathways weakens the muscles that lift the front of the foot, so during the swing phase of gait the toes hang down and catch the ground.

Patients compensate — swinging the leg outward, or lifting the hip to gain clearance — and those compensations are themselves tiring and unstable. The result is a substantially elevated fall risk in a population that is already fragile, and falls after stroke frequently mean fractures and a step backward in recovery.

The standard intervention is a rigid ankle-foot orthosis: a moulded plastic shell that holds the ankle at a fixed angle. It's effective at preventing the toe catch, and it achieves that by removing ankle motion altogether — including the motion the patient is working to recover.

The Design Sprint

Six weeks is not enough time to be precious about anything. The process ran straight through: understand what exists, sketch broadly, build something crude enough to learn from quickly, then commit to a form and test it.

1 — Competitive teardown

Started by examining existing foot drop products to establish what the market already solves well and where it leaves gaps. Rigid AFOs are cheap, reliable, and widely prescribed; their weakness is that they trade away the ankle's range of motion, comfort over long wear, and any capacity to adapt as a patient improves.

2 — Sketching

Broad concept generation before committing to a mechanism — where the device attaches to the leg and foot, how assistance is applied, and how it gets on and off. That last constraint quietly kills otherwise good designs, since many stroke patients have reliable use of only one hand.

3 — Cardboard prototyping

Low-fidelity physical mockups to test geometry and fit before any CAD. Cardboard answers questions about proportion, attachment position, and clearance in an afternoon — questions that take days in a modeling package and can't be answered on screen at all.

4 — CAD

Translating the validated geometry into a manufacturable model, resolving the joint and attachment details the cardboard version had only approximated.

5 — Build and test

Fabricated the prototype and tested it in use, evaluating whether it delivered dorsiflexion assistance through the swing phase without obstructing the rest of the gait cycle.
