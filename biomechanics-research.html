A Test Rig for Predicting Femur Fracture in Osseointegrated Implants

Bone-anchored prosthetics restore natural load transfer to the skeleton. The open question is how much load the remaining femur can take before it breaks — and there is no standard fixture for asking.

Role
Design Engineer & Researcher
Status
Ongoing — third design iteration
Lab
Shefelbine Lab, Northeastern University
Collaborators
Manuela Montes de Oca (PhD candidate) · Dr. Sandra Shefelbine · Dr. Max Shepherd

Why This Matters

In osseointegration, a titanium implant is anchored directly into the residual femur and passes through the skin to connect to an external prosthesis. It eliminates the socket — and with it the skin breakdown, poor fit, and lost proprioception that make traditional limb prostheses hard to live with.

But routing load straight into the bone changes the mechanics of the femur. Stress concentrates around the implant, and periprosthetic fracture is a recognized complication — one that requires revision surgery and can jeopardize the implant entirely. Clinicians need to know how much load a given patient's femur can safely carry, and that answer depends on bone quality, implant geometry, and how deep the stem sits.

Answering it experimentally requires applying physiologically realistic loads to an instrumented femur–implant construct and recording where and when it fails. There is no off-the-shelf fixture that does this. Building one is my thesis.

The Design Problem

A fixture for this test has to satisfy several constraints at once, and they pull against each other:

Physiological loading

Reproduce the load direction and magnitude the femur sees in gait, not just simple axial compression.

Rigid where it counts

The rig must be stiff enough that measured deflection comes from the specimen, not from the fixture flexing.

Repeatable alignment

Specimen-to-specimen variation has to come from the bone, so mounting and alignment must be constrained the same way every time.

Fits the load frame

Bounded by the working envelope and mounting interface of the lab's existing testing machine.

Survives failure

Fracture releases stored energy suddenly. The fixture has to contain that and remain reusable.

Workable specimens

Potting and mounting a bone–implant construct has to be practical for one person to do consistently.

Iterating Toward a Working Fixture

The rig is on its third major revision. Each iteration has been driven by a specific limitation found in the one before it.

Verifying before machining

Fixture components are analyzed in FEA before anything is cut. The goal is to confirm that the structure stays well inside its elastic range under peak test load — if the fixture yields, the data is worthless and the failure mode is ambiguous. Simulating first is considerably cheaper than machining a part that turns out to be undersized.
